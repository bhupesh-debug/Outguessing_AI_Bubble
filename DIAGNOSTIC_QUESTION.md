# The First Diagnostic Question — Outguessing the AI Bubble

This page holds the article's First Diagnostic Question, copied **verbatim** from the companion paper *The First Diagnostic Question — Outguessing the AI Bubble* (Dimri, November 8, 2026). It is **unanswered, by design** — no solution is given here, and none is attempted in this repository. Answer it in your own words, with your reasoning visible, and the coaching program begins from your answer, not from a lecture.

## What this question is testing

The article this question accompanies tests two things at once. First, whether the outguessing framework — reading incentives, anticipating counter-moves, and testing beliefs against evidence — can read a live market that is still moving: the AI market as it stood on November 7, 2026. Second, it tests the reader: the article ends with a single diagnostic question rather than a finished lecture, because outguessing is a skill learned by attempting answers, not by collecting them.

## The book, as far as it can be verified

*Rock Breaks Scissors: A Practical Guide to Outguessing and Outwitting Almost Everybody* was written by William Poundstone and published by Little, Brown and Company in June 2014 (320 pages; ISBN 978-0-316-22806-0), with a paperback edition in September 2015.

The book's premise, in the words of its own dust-jacket summary, is that people are unable to act randomly: instead, they display unconscious patterns that a careful observer can outguess, and the principle applies as much to the crowds that create markets for homes and stocks as to friends playing rock-paper-scissors for a bar tab.

Nothing here reproduces Poundstone's book. The full text was not available to this project, so every claim about the book is either bibliographic fact, publisher or review material, or the book's own dust-jacket summary, and it is labelled as such.

## The First Diagnostic Question

> *Imagine Oracle announces it will raise another $25 billion in bonds to build data centres for a customer whose revenue is a run-rate rather than audited profit, while its own free cash flow is negative. Its shares rise 8% on the news. What do you predict each player actually wants — Oracle's management, the bond buyers, the lab customer, and the rating agencies — what evidence would change your mind, and what is your next move?*

Your answer will be scored on six dimensions: strategic reasoning, probability, bias detection, counterstrategy, calibration, and practical application.

## What happens after you answer

The Socratic sequence: an evaluation of your reasoning, the hidden assumptions it finds, what you did well, your biggest reasoning gap, the strongest counterargument, an expert-level solution, and then a harder scenario. That is how outguessing is learned: one attempted answer at a time, against an opponent who is also thinking.

---

*Glossary for this question: **bond** — a loan an investor makes to a company, repaid with interest. **Run-rate** — revenue projected over a full year from a recent shorter period, as if that pace continued; a projection, not a result. **Audited profit** — profit verified by an independent auditor against the company's accounts. **Free cash flow** — cash left after a business pays its operating costs and its spending on equipment and buildings. **Rating agencies** — independent firms that grade how likely a borrower is to repay its debts.*
