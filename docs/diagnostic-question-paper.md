## What This Article Is Testing
The article this paper accompanies is a test, and it tests two things at once. First, it tests whether the outguessing framework — reading incentives, anticipating counter-moves, and testing beliefs against evidence — can read a live market that is still moving: the artificial-intelligence market as it stood on November 7, 2026. The framework is put to work player by player — NVIDIA, the hyperscalers, Oracle, the two leading labs, and the lenders — with five quantitative tools worked on live questions, a stated hypothesis with conditions that would confirm or falsify it, Monte Carlo simulations run on the research’s own numbers, a SWOT[^2] analysis, six scenarios, a recommendation, and a list of what to avoid. Second, it tests the reader: the article ends with a single diagnostic question rather than a finished lecture, because outguessing is a skill learned by attempting answers, not by collecting them. An everyday way to picture the test: it is like trying a new pair of glasses by reading a street sign at dusk. The question is not whether the glasses look impressive on the shelf, but whether they help you see something real that you could not see before — and whether you can say, in advance, what would prove you wrong.
A word about honesty before we begin, because the agent’s own quality rules demand it. Nothing here reproduces Poundstone’s book. The full text was not available to this project, so every claim about the book is either bibliographic fact, publisher or review material, or the book’s own dust-jacket summary, and it is labeled as such. Every market number in this article comes from the November 7, 2026 research review or from a primary source named in the text; where a figure comes from a secondary summary or a leak, the text says so. Numbers that are not sourced are teaching numbers, and the caption or the text says so. That discipline is not decoration. It is the first lesson of outguessing: separate what you know from what you merely believe, or someone else will do it for you, at your expense.

## The Book, as Far as It Can Be Verified
*Rock Breaks Scissors: A Practical Guide to Outguessing and Outwitting Almost Everybody* was written by William Poundstone and published by Little, Brown and Company in June 2014 (320 pages; ISBN[^3] 978-0-316-22806-0), with a paperback edition in September 2015 (Poundstone, 2014).
The book’s premise, in the words of its own dust-jacket[^4] summary, is that people are unable to act randomly: instead, they display unconscious patterns that a careful observer can outguess, and the principle applies as much to the crowds that create markets for homes and stocks as to friends playing rock-paper-scissors for a bar tab. A contemporary review in *Publishers Weekly* describes the book’s two halves: the first examines situations that look random, from lotteries and computer passwords to Ponzi schemes[^5] and the playground game itself, and argues that what seems random often is not; the second examines the “hot hand[^6]” idea, the belief that winning streaks are predictable, and applies the same skeptical machinery to office football pools and the stock market.
That is the verified core, and it is deliberately modest. The book’s problem is a human one: we are poor at predicting each other, we mistake clusters for patterns and streaks for skill, and we pay for those mistakes in money, negotiations, and votes. Its remedy is equally human: watch what people actually do, learn the shapes their predictability takes, and use probability rather than gut feeling to decide what happens next. The rest of this project treats that modest core as its starting point and labels everything beyond it — established psychology and game theory, and the project’s own teaching examples — as separate from it. ## The First Diagnostic Question
The agent’s design ends its opening overview exactly here: with one question, and a wait. Answer it in your own words, with your reasoning visible, and the coaching program will begin from your answer, not from a lecture. Your answer will be scored on six dimensions: strategic reasoning, probability, bias detection, counterstrategy, calibration, and practical application. No solution is given below, by design.
> *Imagine Oracle announces it will raise another $25 billion in bonds[^7] to build data centres for a customer whose revenue is a run-rate[^8] rather than audited profit[^9], while its own free cash flow[^10] is negative. Its shares rise 8% on the news. What do you predict each player actually wants — Oracle’s management, the bond buyers, the lab customer, and the rating agencies[^11] — what evidence would change your mind, and what is your next move?*
Reply with your answer whenever you are ready. The next step after that is the agent’s Socratic sequence: an evaluation of your reasoning, the hidden assumptions it finds, what you did well, your biggest reasoning gap, the strongest counterargument, an expert-level solution, and then a harder scenario. That is how outguessing is learned: one attempted answer at a time, against an opponent who is also thinking.

## Glossary
The terms below are the key terms used in this paper. Each is listed with its full form where it has one, and a short definition in plain language. Terms marked with a footnote number are also defined in a footnote at their first mention in the text.

- **AI** (artificial intelligence) — Computer systems built to do tasks that usually need human intelligence, such as learning from data and making predictions.
- **audited profit** — Profit checked and verified by an independent auditor against the company’s accounts.
- **bond** — A loan an investor makes to a company or government, repaid with interest over an agreed period.
- **calibration** — How well a person’s stated confidence matches how often they turn out to be right.
- **circular deal** — A deal in which money or commitments flow in a loop between related parties, so the same value is counted more than once.
- **counterstrategy** — A move made in response to an opponent’s move, anticipating what they will do next.
- **diagnostic question** — The single question that ends the opening overview and starts the coaching; the reader’s answer, not a lecture, is what the program builds on.
- **dust-jacket** — The printed paper cover wrapped around a hardcover book, carrying its title and author.
- **free cash flow** — The cash a business has left after paying its operating costs and its spending on equipment and buildings.
- **hot hand** — The belief that a run of successes makes further success more likely; the book’s second half tests whether winning streaks are really predictable.
- **ISBN** (International Standard Book Number) — The unique number printed on a published book, identifying its exact edition.
- **leveraged** — Built on borrowed money; a leveraged deal uses debt to fund an investment larger than the cash put in.
- **Monte Carlo simulation** — A calculation that runs the same situation thousands of times with random inputs drawn from stated distributions, to show the range of possible outcomes.
- **outguessing** — Reading what people actually do, learning the shapes their predictability takes, and anticipating their next move before they make it.
- **Ponzi scheme** — A fraud in which money from new investors is used to pay earlier investors, until no new money arrives and the scheme collapses.
- **rating agencies** — Independent firms that grade how likely a borrower is to repay its debts; their grades decide how much borrowing costs.
- **run-rate** — Revenue projected over a full year from a recent shorter period, as if that period’s pace continued; a projection, not a result.
- **Socratic method** — Teaching by asking questions, so the learner reaches the answer by reasoning rather than by being told.
- **SWOT** (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) — A framework that sorts what helps and what hurts a plan into four boxes: two internal (strengths, weaknesses) and two external (opportunities, threats).
