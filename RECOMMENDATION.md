# Recommendation — Outguessing the AI Bubble

The recommendation follows from the hypothesis and the simulations, and it is a position, not a prediction. **Nothing here is investment advice; it is a reading of incentives, with the evidence that would prove it wrong attached.**

Source: *Outguessing the AI Bubble* (Dimri, Nov 8, 2026), "Recommendation" and "What to Avoid" (Tables 6 and 10 in the article).

## In one sentence

> Treat the core and the tail as different assets, own the workflow rather than the model call, and let the markers — not the headlines — decide when the thesis changes.

## The recommendation — seven actions

### 1. Separate the core from the tail, and size the tail so the bad branch is survivable

The Monte Carlo is the arithmetic behind this: the leveraged tail's median 12-month outcome is fine ($102.29 on $100), and its chance of losing more than a fifth is 31.7 percent, against 0.3 percent for a diversified position. That asymmetry is the whole recommendation. Whatever exposure you hold to the leveraged tail — Oracle, neoclouds, supplier-guaranteed deals — size it so that the 5th-percentile outcome ($34.90 on $100) is a bad year, not a ruinous one.

### 2. Own the workflow, the data, or the distribution — not the model call

The survivors in the research review are the products that own workflow state, proprietary data, integrations, or a channel the model provider cannot ship as a checkbox: Cursor, Claude Code, Harvey. The casualties are the products whose entire value was a system prompt.

### 3. Make cost a first-class metric

Choose the smallest model that passes an evaluation; cache, batch, and measure tokens per task; keep a cost ceiling and a kill-switch in every product. Inference now dominates training cost over a model's life, and the developer who can cut a bill tenfold without losing quality is more valuable than the one who can call the most expensive model.

### 4. Diversify providers and skills

Learn open models alongside closed APIs, and keep the evaluation that lets you prove one is good enough to swap. Do not tie a career or a product to a single lab, a single cloud, or a single framework: the labs are each other's customers, competitors, and investors, and that concentration is the systemic risk.

### 5. Watch the markers, not the headlines

The six markers in the article's Table 5 (see [HYPOTHESIS.md](HYPOTHESIS.md)), three or more on either side together; the single most informative is the first audited OpenAI and Anthropic prospectus. Credit spreads move first and headlines move second, so watch the spreads, Oracle's free cash flow, and the backlog conversion — not the press releases.

### 6. Stage decisions and pre-commit the exits

Decide in advance what evidence would change your mind — the Hypothesis section's confirm-or-refute lists are the template — and write down the exit condition before you enter, not during a sell-off. A decision made in advance is a strategy; the same decision made in panic is a reaction.

### 7. Keep a core skill that is employable with or without an AI boom

Software, data, or cloud work that stands on its own. If the tail reprices, hiring tightens first in the leveraged tail — neoclouds, labs, wrapper startups — and last in the profitable core; a core skill is the hedge that does not depend on being right about the market.

## What to avoid — ten traps

Most of the money lost in a bubble is not lost to the bubble. It is lost to the avoidable mistakes made inside it. Each trap below is from the article's Table 10: the trap, why it hurts, and the move that replaces it.

| # | Trap | Why it hurts | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Buying the story instead of the base rate | Narrative momentum punishes early scepticism, but base rates punish late belief harder. Cisco fell 86–90% and never regained its 2000 peak. | Demand a probability-weighted case and a named base rate before deciding. |
| 2 | Mistaking run-rate for booked revenue | Run-rates annualise a good month; GAAP revenue in a prospectus cannot. The $70bn OpenAI run-rate was investor arithmetic, not a company disclosure. | Wait for the S-1; treat run-rates as marketing until audited. |
| 3 | Mistaking announced figures for signed ones | NVIDIA's $100bn OpenAI letter of intent was never signed and was replaced by $30bn of equity; headlines count the bigger number. | Separate announced from signed from cash actually spent. |
| 4 | Mistaking backlog for cash | Oracle's $455bn RPO and Microsoft's $627bn backlog are promises, not cash; they convert only if the customer's revenue arrives. | Track conversion — free cash flow and recognised revenue — not the backlog print. |
| 5 | Building a business on a subsidy | Free tiers, flat-rate plans, and cloud credits tighten; a subsidy is not a margin. Cursor, Anthropic, and OpenAI all tightened in 2025–26. | Design for pay-per-token from day one, with a cost ceiling and a kill-switch. |
| 6 | Building a thin wrapper | If the lab ships your feature natively tomorrow, nothing is left. Jasper's revenue fell 54%; Builder.ai went bankrupt. | Own workflow state, proprietary data, integrations, or distribution. |
| 7 | Tying yourself to one provider | The labs are each other's customers, competitors, and investors; concentration is the systemic risk. | Learn open models alongside closed APIs; keep the evaluation that lets you swap. |
| 8 | Ignoring the credit market | Credit spreads move first and headlines move second. Oracle's CDS reached its highest since 2008–09 while the equity story was still being told. | Watch spreads, downgrades, and free cash flow, not press releases. |
| 9 | False precision | A probability carried to two decimal places on a guessed prior is theatre. Figure 4's 40% is a teaching number, not a market price. | State your inputs, your ranges, and what would flip the answer. |
| 10 | Treating a streak as a law | The hot-hand error: mistaking a winning streak for a predictable pattern (Gilovich et al., 1985). | Ask for the base rate and the sample size before extrapolating a streak. |

## Conclusion

The evidence supports a narrow verdict, and the narrowness is the point. A bubble is forming in the financing of AI infrastructure — about $1.4 trillion of future revenue commitments raised against about $950 billion of equity and debt, 55.2 percent of AI investment coming from other AI firms, a leveraged tail borrowing to build capacity whose end-user revenue has not yet arrived. It is not yet proven to be near popping, because the revenue is real and accelerating, the core spenders are highly profitable, and the technology gets cheaper by the month. The Monte Carlo runs put numbers on that tension: the lab sector most likely covers its compute from revenue by 2029, but nearly one run in five does not; the 2026 hyperscaler build is mostly not self-funding on guidance; and the leveraged tail's median outcome is fine while its downside is a cliff. The honest position is therefore neither alarm nor complacency: size the tail so the bad branch is survivable, own the workflow rather than the model call, and watch the markers — the prospectuses, Oracle's cash, and the credit spreads — because they will decide the argument before the headlines do. The market will tell you what it is doing, if you watch what it does rather than what it says. That is the whole of outguessing, and it is enough.
